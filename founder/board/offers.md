# Offers lens

Source applied: the "Offers" lens in `lenses.md` (a summary of the framework in *$100M Offers*). This memo speaks as the framework, not as its author.

**Score: 6 / 10 | Vote: FUND IF**

## What is strong

**The market passes three of the four tests.** Massive pain — yes. A delegate's prep week is genuinely unpleasant: committee briefs, country positions, a position paper, an opening speech and POI prep, all due before a date that cannot move, on topics they may never have studied. Buyers with money — yes, or close to it: the same delegate, or their school, has already committed **₹2,500 to register for the conference itself** (Delhi MUN's published fee). A **₹299** prep tool is 12% of the entry fee to the thing it helps them win. That is the single most important number in this analysis: the anchor is already set and it is an order of magnitude above the price. Easy to find and reach — yes, unusually so. MUN delegates cluster hard: school clubs, delegation WhatsApp groups, MUN Instagram pages, and a public calendar of conferences. They are reachable in bulk, by hand, for free. Growing — yes; conferences are multiplying across Indian cities.

**The dream outcome is real and close.** "Walk into committee prepared, win an award, don't get embarrassed by a bad statistic in a moderated caucus." That is a high-status, emotionally loaded outcome in a competitive adolescent social world.

**The price is not the objection.** ₹299 sits below ChatGPT Go (₹399) and far below Model Diplomat Pro ($20 ≈ ₹1,760/month). Cheap is not the constraint here.

## What would kill it — the top three, most dangerous first

**1. The value equation fails on *perceived likelihood*, and the failure is structural, not fixable with better copy.** The offer asks a delegate to stake their position paper and opening speech on text a model produced with **no retrieval layer and no citations** — while the competitor's entire pitch is that it *cannot* cite a document outside its index, and explicitly contrasts itself with "does it invent citations like ChatGPT? No." A delegate who quotes a hallucinated statistic in a moderated caucus is humiliated in front of their committee. Perceived likelihood of getting the outcome — not getting burned — is therefore low, and the risk is highest exactly where the product is most useful. The system prompt's "mark it as needs verification" instruction is a partial patch on a structural gap, and it makes the product look uncertain rather than authoritative.

**2. Effort and sacrifice are being asked of the buyer at the wrong moment.** The buyer must (a) discover the site, (b) create a Google account sign-in, (c) *pay before seeing any output*, and (d) get zero proof it is any better than the ChatGPT they already have open in the next tab. There is **no free tier and no free trial**, and the price is hidden behind the sign-in wall. The competitor gives 200 searches and then 50/day free — it lets the delegate feel the outcome before asking for money. Asking for ₹199 before a single demonstration, into a market that has a free alternative one tap away, suppresses the outcome's perceived value to near zero for a cold buyer. This is a distribution-and-proof failure more than a price failure, and it is the most fixable of the three.

**3. It is priced against a commodity, not an outcome.** Three modes and a token slider is a *feature* comparison against ChatGPT. Nothing in the offer stack is yet scarce or proprietary: no indexed sources, no exclusive past-paper bank, no coach's playbook, no community. If the only differentiator a buyer can perceive is "MUN-shaped prompts," this competes on price, and the lens is blunt about where that ends.

## The questions I would ask the founder

1. If a delegate only has ₹299 of prep budget and ChatGPT is already open, in one sentence — what does your tool do that ChatGPT cannot, that the delegate would notice in the first five minutes?
2. Can you show a citation-free guarantee you can actually honour? If not, what is your answer when a chair asks "where is that from?"
3. What is your proof that the output is good? Is there one delegate, one award, one screenshot of "this won me Best Delegate?"
4. Why is there no trial, when the competitor's free tier is the thing doing the selling?
5. What is the guarantee? If the delegate walks into committee and the brief is useless, what do they get back?

## If FUND IF: the conditions

1. **Put a demonstration before the payment.** A free tier or trial with a hard cap (e.g. 5 AI requests, once per account, no card) whose only job is to show output quality. Ship before spending a rupee on acquisition.
2. **Show the price before the sign-in.** `/pricing` redirecting to `/auth/signin` costs every cold visitor.
3. **Answer the hallucination objection inside the product**, not in the prompt: surface sources, or ship a "verify before you speak" artefact as a *feature* (a checklist with the specific claims that need checking, plus where to check them), and sell it as rigour rather than apologising for a gap.
4. **Build the stack around the conference deadline, not around tokens.** The buyer's clock is a date. Sell "conference in 5 days" a burnt-down prep plan; add a real, dated launch-week bonus; a realistic guarantee tied to the prep week.
5. **Get one piece of third-party proof** — an award, a testimonial the delegate actually said, a coach's endorsement — and put it on the page. Until then the offer is unproven, and free rivals are one tab away.
6. **Do not answer the ChatGPT objection with "more MUN features."** Answer it with rigour you can evidence (citations or verifiable claims) plus speed to a *conference-ready* artefact.
